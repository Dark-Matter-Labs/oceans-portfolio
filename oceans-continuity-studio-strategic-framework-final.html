01 — The Why: Meeting the moment

The ocean has quietly become load-bearing infrastructure for modern civilisation. The systems built to govern it were designed for a time when it was not.

For most of the industrial era the ocean was understood in two ways: an environment to protect, or a frontier to extract from. Both are now insufficient. Offshore energy is becoming a cornerstone of decarbonisation; subsea cables carry the overwhelming majority of global data; fisheries and marine agriculture underwrite food security for billions; marine biodiversity is a frontier for pharmaceutical and material discovery; and the ocean regulates the climate system that agriculture, insurance, and coastlines depend on. The ocean is no longer peripheral. It is a continuity infrastructure — the substrate through which resilience, provisioning, and systemic risk are increasingly organised.

Two forces now move against each other, which we describe as the:

01

Ocean Provision Stack

The layered systems through which the ocean provisions the modern human economy, and are growing increasingly load-bearing as societies lean on them harder.
Biomaterials — algae, seaweed, deep-sea systems replacing fossil-based inputs
Bionutrients & food — fisheries and marine agriculture feeding billions
Biopharma discovery — marine molecular diversity for medicine
Energy & data — offshore renewables, subsea cable networks
Life Search Option Space — biodiversity as an irreplaceable discovery reservoir
02

Ocean Shock Cascades

The escalating ocean-origin failures, climatic and ecological, that cross critical thresholds and transmit outward into the food, coastal, and climate systems that depend on the sea.
Sea-level rise — re-baselining coastal risk from rare to persistent
Flood & surge — compound failure across drainage, aquifers, logistics
Storm intensification — atmospheric rivers, heavier extremes
Ecosystem destabilisation — warming, acidification, deoxygenation
Infrastructure exposure — cables, energy arrays, ports at rising risk

The problem is not a shortage of concern or capital. It is structural. Governance is fragmented across sectors and bounded by national jurisdiction, while ecological systems cross both by design. Verification is thin, so disputes over ecological fact escalate into political ones. Finance is built for short horizons and cannot price ecological integrity or capitalise public goods. Each institution behaves rationally inside its own mandate, yet the ocean is not divisible into those mandates. The result is a system that defaults toward fragmentation and extraction — not because anyone chooses it, but because the machinery currently in place cannot choose anything else.

Closing that gap is not an environmental ambition layered on top of the economy. It is a precondition for the stability of the wealth, provisioning, and public systems that depend on the ocean.

That is the work the Oceans Continuity Studio is being constituted to do.

Provision × Shock. Empirical work already links the two: analysing disasters across 104 countries and 27 market indices, Pagnottoni et al. (2022) found biological and climatic shocks exerted the strongest effects on financial markets.

Ecological disruption is a financial-stability question, not only a planetary one.
Keystone map: what is actually at stake

The stakes are not diffuse. A small number of marine systems are load-bearing for human stability — keystone not because they are large, but because the failure of each transmits outward through food, markets, migration, coastline, and climate into large-population destabilisation.

02 — The studio

A venture studio, not a fund and not an advocacy body: it builds the missing institutions directly, resources them through a shared capital stack, and places operators inside what it builds.

The Oceans Continuity Studio (OCS) builds ventures the way a venture studio builds companies — a repeatable formation process, a shared build capability drawn on across the whole portfolio, staged capital matched to what has been proven, and operators embedded in each venture rather than supervising from outside. Where a conventional venture studio spins out companies, this one spins out the governance bodies, legal instruments, data infrastructure, and financing facilities that ocean systems need and that no existing institution is positioned to build.

The reason it takes the studio form is the political-economy lock-in named above. The missing institutions are transnational, cross-sectoral, and long-horizon; they do not emerge from any single incumbent because every incumbent is structured to optimise its own mandate. A studio can hold the whole picture — assembling actors who would not otherwise coordinate, carrying capability between contexts, and staging capital so that early, catalytic money de-risks the later, larger money that follows. It is a capitalisation and coordination architecture for continuity, built to work at the scale the problem actually operates at.

03 — The architecture

Four capability domains, a portfolio of place-based options, and shared build capability between them. Read the four terms once and the rest of this document follows the same geometry.

While the studio holds overarching portfolio design and orchestration functions, its internal architecture is structured around four intersecting, mutually constitutive elements:

Necessary capabilities Domain One of (currently) four capabilities required to close the structural gap outlined in Section 01. A domain may constitute a lab to develop replicable capabilities.
Transversal experimentation Lab A self-contained entity within the studio building replicable domain-specific capabilities in service of the entire options portfolio.
Place-based outcomes portfolio Option A concrete body of place or context specific work in response to one or more marine challenges.
Option-specific instrumentation Intervention The named piece of work where a domain meets an option: what gets funded, staffed, and delivered.
Distribution of responsibilities

The studio holds what is constitutive of the whole; a lab holds what is replicable across places; an option holds what is constituted by its place.

The studio holds what is constitutive Portfolio design and orchestration, thesis and definitions; the capital stack and its sequencing; the templates and formats every option would otherwise rebuild; coordination and continuity through partner turnover. Founding layer · DML + xCO
A lab holds what is replicable A self-contained build entity on one or more domain areas: it builds and versions domain-specific instruments for transversal application across the options portfolio, and trains the operators who carry them into place. A domain may run one lab, several, or none. CONSORTIUM, HELD BY LAB STEWARD
An option holds what is local The constituted body, the relationships, the politics of one marine system, and the translation of each instrument into that place. CONSORTIUM, HELD BY ANCHOR PARTNER
The four domains

Building continuity infrastructure for marine systems required consolidated intervention across four domain areas.

Note: These are subject to evolve as our understanding of the risk field expands.

The gap: fragmented, jurisdiction-bound, thinly-verified, short-horizon governance
Domain 1 Data, sensing & legibility Makes marine and non-human systems readable as evidence a court, regulator, or insurer can act on. Closes: verification & knowledge asymmetry. Hands on: evidence, to a fixed specification. ▪ Multispecies Ontologies Lab
Domain 2 Standing & litigation Converts that evidence into legal standing and shared truth conditions that narrow the space for dispute. Closes: fragmented, contested authority. Hands on: a claim, and parties bound to the same facts. ▪ Oceans Diplomacy Lab
Domain 3 Governance infrastructure Gives the claim somewhere to be decided continuously and across jurisdictions, rather than at periodic negotiation. Closes: jurisdictional mismatch. Hands on: a body that can hold decisions over time.
Domain 4 Systemic financing Funds the other three and converts verified outcomes into claims that capital can actually hold. Closes: financial misalignment. Hands on: capital, and a reason for it to keep coming.

How the architecture grows

The current portfolio addresses a specific slice of the ocean problem. The keystone map in Section 01 shows the rest of it — load-bearing systems whose failure transmits into food, migration, coastline, and climate. Each is a candidate future option; each may surface capability needs that justify a new lab, or a domain the current four do not cover.

One continuous picture. The matrix described above is an indicative spread for Phase 1 of OCS. The keystone map is where the structure could extend next.
New optionA system becomes an optionWhen work in a marine system is concrete enough to resource and a coalition exists to hold it, it enters the portfolio as a new option cutting across all identified domains.
New labA capability becomes a labWhen a capability, tool, or method is identified as being replicable across options, a lab may be constituted to build transversal instruments to a shared interoperable specification.
New functionA need becomes a ringfenced entityWhen the portfolio needs a function the current structure does not contain, the studio creates a dedicated function to hold it — with its own remit and resourcing.

04 — The options

An option is a concrete body of work in one marine system, held by a coalition, cutting across all four domains. This is the current roster and what each is for.

Each option assembles the actors already active in a marine system around a shared body of work running across all four domains — data and legibility, standing and litigation, governance, and financing. An option is not a single project or instrument; it is the full cross-domain stack applied to one system, held by a coalition and stewarded by an anchor partner end to end. Options differ in their organising unit i.e. the wager each makes about what scale governance should actually operate at.

Coalition, not client. An option is held by the actors already moving in a system — the studio finds what is moving, connects who is stirring, and supplies the capital, method, and coordination the work needs to take root. Each carries its own concept paper as it matures.
option 1 PILOT North Sea / Doggerlands — Marine Regenerative Area organising unit: marine bioregion A densely-used, multi-jurisdiction seascape where energy, shipping, fisheries, and data infrastructure already overlap — governed as one ecological system rather than a patchwork of national zones. Potential Consortium — Embassy of the North Sea (Anchor Partner) · Doggerland Foundation · Katapult Ocean · Bioregional Weaving Lab
option 2 In active development Whale Nation organising unit: species / more-than-human sovereignty A wager that the unit of governance can be a species rather than a territory: legal standing, fiduciary representation, and restoration-backed finance organised around whales as sovereign subjects. Potential Consortium — Naia Trust (Anchor Partner), Pacific Whales Fund Read: The Whale and the State →
option 3 Emerging Antarctic Sovereignty Initiative organising unit: territorial sovereignty A wager on governance organized around a shared-sovereignty territory rather than a single ecological unit. Potential Consortium — Impact Trust · Nature on Board
option 4 Emerging Baltic Sea — Marine Regenerative Area organising unit: marine bioregion The second/parallel bioregional option, and the first test of replication — reusing the North Sea model on a different sea with its own coalition and ecological profile. Potential Consortium — Embassy of the Baltic Sea (Anchor Partner) · Katapult Ocean · Bioregional Weaving Lab

As new marine systems from the keystone map are taken on, they enter here as new options against the same structure.

05 — The labs

This section outlines what a lab is made of, how an option draws an instrument down, and the current roster.

A lab is three things held together: a versioned portfolio of versioned domain-specific instruments and capabilities, a consortium of technical partners who maintain and extend it, and a lab steward i.e. an anchor organisation that convenes the consortium and holds it accountable to the studio's thesis.

06 — The capital stack

Combinatorial by design. The stack is built to take risk on combinatorial systems — several interacting functions financed as one operating environment.
Capital pooling

Capital enters the studio through one of two tracks:

Track 1Shared PoolFunds the studio itself i.e. the full portfolio, labs, shared methodology, coordination function, etc. — from catalytic, mission-aligned capital.
Track 2Option or Lab-Specific PoolFunds a specific option or lab. The studio ensures this runs through a shared protocol, so that capital aimed at one option still produces outputs comparable across the portfolio.
Capital stacking

Within either track, capital is stacked across four horizons:

H4
Institutional asset layer — takeout capitalMarket-compatible instruments issued once outcomes are verified — the layer at which continuity work becomes ordinary investable infrastructure. Liquidity: instrument-specific. Nothing in the portfolio has reached issuance yet; this is the horizon the earlier layers build toward.
H3
Development capital — strategic, risk-onStaged, conditional capital that enters as an option proves out and scales what works. Expects a return but tolerates the risk of an unproven model. Liquidity: controlled transfer, no redemption assumption.
H2
Mission equity — patient capitalSeed capital exchanged for responsibility to develop an option and standing accountability to the mission — not ownership, not a fixed deliverable. Released in tranches against milestones; where an option underperforms the response is graduated — continue, pivot, merge, pause, close, or transfer stewardship — never an automatic clawback and never silent tolerance. Liquidity: illiquid, restricted transfer.
H1
Mission protection — catalytic / philanthropic capitalThe earliest and most patient layer. Funds the verification rails, shared methodology, and baseline measurement that make an option investable by anything above it. Absorbs first loss. Liquidity: none — non-redeemable.

07 — The formation sequence

One option and one lab first, to prove the model works — before capital or attention moves anywhere else. Four stages, run once, then repeated.

The studio proves its architecture on a single pairing before extending it: North Sea as the pilot option, Multispecies Ontologies as the pilot lab. The pairing is deliberate — a bioregion with a coalition already forming, matched to the one capability furthest along.

Pilot optionNorth Sea – Marine Regenerative AreaPotential Consortium — Embassy of the North Sea (Anchor Partner) · Doggerland Foundation · Katapult Ocean · Bioregional Weaving Lab

Every option runs across these four stages:

01
Seed

One option, one lab. Assemble the coalition around a first concrete capability and prove the shared protocol works in a live context.

Capital: H1
02
Local anchoring

Formalise the anchor partner on mission-equity terms; place the first operator; stand up the governance body as a real decision-making forum.

Capital: H1 + first H2 tranche
03
Construction

Build the option's instruments across the remaining domains.

Capital: H2 fully tranched, first H3 entry
04
Self-sustaining operation

The coalition runs under its own governance. The studio's role shifts to replication, and the documented model seeds the next option.

Capital: H3 active, H4 viable once verified

Once the pilot reaches self-sustaining operation, the next options reuse its documented model with local adaptation, and the studio begins entering the expansion horizon from Section 03 — new systems, and where justified new labs and functions — one proven step at a time. Options already further along on a particular capability feed it back into the shared labs, so the portfolio compounds what each proves rather than starting each from zero.

08 — The partner model

Three tiers of organisational relationship, plus a placed-operator mechanism running through them. Relationships attach per-relationship, not per-organisation.
Tier 1 · per option or lab Anchor Partner
Holds — commitment over one option or lab end to end.
Receives — mission equity (§06, H2): staged seed capital for responsibility; its operator's access to the labs; standing in a recognised portfolio.
Produces — outputs in the shared format; reporting reviewed on a fixed cadence against the milestones its capital was released for; hosting a placed operator.
Tier 2 · per intervention Contributing Partner
Holds — one or more intervention inside an option, or a seat in a lab's consortium.
Receives — access to the relevant slice of the lab's instrument; attribution; a targeted allocation scoped to that piece.
Produces — design outcomes, facilitation as needed, reporting on that piece, to whoever it contributes under.
Brought in by — the anchor partner or lab steward under delegated authority; not every relationship routes through the centre.
Tier 3 · capital Funding Partner
Holds — a capital position.
Receives — reporting and impact narrative, delivered collectively rather than as an individual governance seat.
Distinct from — the founding layer, which holds authorship and the earliest capital; a larger check does not convert one role into the other.

Relationships attach per-relationship, not per-organisation: the same partner can fund the studio overall and separately anchor or contribute to one option.
