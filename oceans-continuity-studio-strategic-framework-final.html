01 — The why: meeting the moment

The ocean has quietly become load-bearing infrastructure for modern civilisation. The systems built to govern it were designed for a time when it was not.

For most of the industrial era the ocean was understood in two ways: an environment to protect, or a frontier to extract from. Both are now insufficient. Offshore energy is becoming a cornerstone of decarbonisation; subsea cables carry the overwhelming majority of global data; fisheries and marine agriculture underwrite food security for billions; marine biodiversity is a frontier for pharmaceutical and material discovery; and the ocean regulates the climate system that agriculture, insurance, and coastlines depend on. The ocean is no longer peripheral. It is a continuity infrastructure — the substrate through which resilience, provisioning, and systemic risk are increasingly organised.

Two forces now move against each other —

Ocean Provision Stack

Bionutrients & food — fisheries and marine agriculture feeding billions

02

Ocean Shock Cascades

The escalating ocean-origin failures, climatic and ecological, that cross critical thresholds and transmit outward into the food, coastal, and climate systems that depend on the sea.
Sea-level rise — re-baselining coastal risk from rare to persistent
Flood & surge — compound failure across drainage, aquifers, logistics
Storm intensification — atmospheric rivers, heavier extremes
Ecosystem destabilisation — warming, acidification, deoxygenation
Infrastructure exposure — cables, energy arrays, ports at rising risk

The problem is not a shortage of concern or capital. It is structural:

  • Governance is fragmented across sectors and bounded by national jurisdiction, while ecological systems cross both by design.
  • Verification is thin, so disputes over ecological fact escalate into political ones.
  • Finance is built for short horizons and cannot price ecological integrity or capitalise public goods.

Each institution behaves rationally inside its own mandate, yet the ocean is not divisible into those mandates. The result is a system that defaults toward fragmentation and extraction. Closing that gap is not an environmental ambition layered on top of the economy. It is a precondition for the stability of the wealth, provisioning, and public systems that depend on the ocean.

That is the work the Oceans Continuity Studio is being constituted to do.

Provision × Shock. Empirical work already links the two: analysing disasters across 104 countries and 27 market indices, Pagnottoni et al. (2022) found biological and climatic shocks exerted the strongest effects on financial markets.

Ecological disruption is a financial-stability question, not only a planetary one.

02 — The studio

A venture studio, not a fund and not an advocacy body: it builds the missing institutions directly, resources them through a shared capital stack, and places operators inside what it builds.

The Oceans Continuity Studio (OCS) builds ventures the way a venture studio builds companies — a repeatable formation process, a shared build capability drawn on across the whole portfolio, staged capital matched to what has been proven, and operators embedded in each venture rather than supervising from outside. Where a conventional venture studio spins out companies, this one spins out the governance bodies, legal instruments, data infrastructure, and financing facilities that ocean systems need and that no existing institution is positioned to build.

The reason it takes the studio form is the political-economy lock-in named above. The missing institutions are transnational, cross-sectoral, and long-horizon; they do not emerge from any single incumbent because every incumbent is structured to optimise its own mandate. A studio can hold the whole picture — assembling actors who would not otherwise coordinate, carrying capability between contexts, and staging capital so that early, catalytic money de-risks the later, larger money that follows. It is a capitalisation and coordination architecture for continuity, built to work at the scale the problem actually operates at.

How the architecture grows

The current portfolio addresses a specific slice of the ocean problem. The keystone map in Section 01 shows the rest of it — load-bearing systems whose failure transmits into food, migration, coastline, and climate. Each is a candidate future option; each may surface capability needs that justify a new lab, or a domain the current four do not cover.

One continuous picture. The matrix described above is an provisional spread for Phase 1 of the OCS portfolio.
New optionA system becomes an optionWhen work in a marine system is concrete enough to resource and a coalition exists to hold it, it enters the portfolio as a new option cutting across all identified domains.
New labA capability becomes a labWhen a capability, tool, or method is identified as being replicable across options, a lab may be constituted to build transversal instruments to a shared interoperable specification.
New functionA need becomes a ringfenced entityWhen the portfolio needs a function the current structure does not contain, the studio creates a dedicated function to hold it — with its own remit and resourcing.

04 — The options

An option is a concrete body of work in one marine system, held by a coalition, cutting across all four domains. This section outlines the current portfolio of options.

An option is not a single project or instrument: it is the full cross-domain stack applied to one (or more) keystone marine system, held by a consortium and stewarded by an anchor partner end-to-end. The consortium involves the actors already moving in a system; the studio finds what is moving, connects who is stirring, and supplies the capital, method, and coordination the work needs to take root. Options differ in their organising unit i.e. the wager each makes about what scale governance should actually operate at.

06 — The capital logic chain

How capital is raised, organised, and allocated across the studio, its labs, and its options.

The studio operates through three units: its own founding layer, its labs, and its options. Each produces different assets on different horizons and is judged by different evidence — coordination capacity that no single place can be billed for; replicable capability whose value is only realised in the options that later adopt it; and place-specific portfolios whose returns are long-dated and, at the outset, unpriceable. Capital is therefore raised into three pools, each coded to one unit and matched to the horizon and return that unit can honestly offer.

The three pools
Pool 01 · most upstream Existential / Systemic Mission Equity Capital Capitalises the studio
Docks as — membership in the studio itself Funds the studio's formation: sensing and mapping the risk field, option selection, the capital architecture itself, governance, and coordination through partner turnover. This is the capital that has to exist before anything downstream is bankable.
InstrumentMission Equity — membership in the studio entity plus contractual rights. No shares, no distributable capital.
ReturnNon-direct. Governance standing, information and proximity rights, preferential first-look into Pools 02 and 03. Risk-in logic: capital protects the field it depends on, rather than betting on a return.
Rights mechanicsPreferential first-look rights into Pool 03 are not negotiated deal-by-deal. They are embedded directly in the Mission Equity instrument itself — holding it is what carries the standing right, for as long as the membership is active.
HorizonIndefinite, mission-locked. The first wave is explicitly non-redeemable and non-repayable.
Risk framingRisk-in: it protects the field everything downstream depends on.
Who docks hereLearning partners, then mission equity partners, then civilisation-scale and sovereign partners — capital holders backing the studio rather than any one place.
“You’re not backing an option. You’re capitalising the studio that makes any ocean option investable at all.”
Pool 02 · the connective layer Capability Capital Seeds a lab
Docks as — a build stake in one named lab Seeds and sustains a lab as a standing entity, not a single output: its research and development function, its operations, the consortium that maintains and extends it, the stewardship that holds it to the studio's thesis, and the training of the operators who carry its instruments into place. This is the machinery that makes options executable.
InstrumentTwo forms. Portfolio-level: a fund across the labs being built. Or direct: investment straight into one named lab and the instruments it builds. Either way, tranched against build and adoption milestones over a standing operating base, so the research function does not stall between tranches.
ReturnDepends on maturity, not on the pool. A lab still building reads as capability return — protocol and IP standing, reusable infrastructure, not yet a priced asset. An instrument adopted across several options reads closer to conventional return, tied to that instrument’s own uptake.
HorizonMedium, and maturity-dependent — earlier-stage labs run longer and closer to Pool 01's patience. On the lab's own build-and-version cycle, not any one option's clock.
Risk framingBuild risk: whether what the lab builds is adopted and reused beyond the option it was first built for.
Who docks hereThe lab steward and the technical consortium it convenes; mission-aligned foundations doing venture-philanthropy; capital holders who want the capability more than any one place.
“This is the machinery, not the place. Build it once, and every option after the first draws it down instead of rebuilding it.”
Pool 03 · most downstream Option-level / First Participation Capital Seeds an option
Docks as — the facility itself, at one named marine system Funds concrete interventions on the ground: the full portfolio an option runs across every domain, and the institutions that hold them — the governance body that makes binding decisions, the legal capacity that defends them, and the financing facility that eventually places capital on that system's own account. The most legible entry point in the whole chain.
InstrumentPatient, milestone-tranched capital while the coalition and its portfolio are built, with a graduated response if it underperforms — continue, pivot, merge, pause, close, or transfer stewardship; never a silent tolerance, never an automatic clawback. Converts to the option's own market-compatible instrument once that facility is verified.
ReturnScales with proof: governance standing and milestone credit while proving, direct financial return once the named facility issues. Paid, ultimately, by the actor who already carries the exposure the intervention reduces.
HorizonIlliquid while proving; facility tenor once issuing. Nothing in the portfolio has reached issuance yet.
Risk framingCoalition risk early, market risk late.
Who docks hereAn anchor partner and its option consortium; the named risk-holders whose own exposure that system's facility reduces; institutional and fund actors once the instrument is standardised.
“This is the place you can actually point to. Direct standing in a real coalition, with the return maturing as the model proves out.”

07 — The formation sequence

One option and one lab first, to prove the model works — before capital or attention moves anywhere else. Four stages, run once, then repeated.

The studio proves its architecture on a single pairing before extending it: North Sea as the pilot option, Multispecies Ontologies as the pilot lab. The pairing is deliberate — a bioregion with a coalition already forming, matched to the one capability furthest along.

08 — The partner model

Three tiers of organisational relationship, plus a placed-operator mechanism running through them. Relationships attach per-relationship, not per-organisation.
Tier 1 · per option or lab Anchor Partner
Holds — commitment over one option or lab end to end.
Receives — option capital (§06, Pool 03): patient, milestone-tranched capital while the coalition and its instruments are built; its operator's access to the labs; standing in a recognised portfolio.
Produces — outputs in the shared format; reporting reviewed on a fixed cadence against the milestones its capital was released for; hosting a placed operator.
Tier 2 · per intervention Contributing Partner
Holds — one or more intervention inside an option, or a seat in a lab's consortium.
Receives — access to the relevant slice of the lab's instrument; attribution; a targeted allocation scoped to that piece.
Produces — design outcomes, facilitation as needed, reporting on that piece, to whoever it contributes under.
Brought in by — the anchor partner or lab steward under delegated authority; not every relationship routes through the centre.
Tier 3 · capital Funding Partner
Holds — a capital position.
Receives — reporting and impact narrative, delivered collectively rather than as an individual governance seat.
Distinct from — the founding layer, which holds authorship and the earliest capital; a larger check does not convert one role into the other.

Relationships attach per-relationship, not per-organisation: the same partner can fund the studio overall and separately anchor or contribute to one option.
